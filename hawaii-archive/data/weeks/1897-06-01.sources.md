# Hawaiʻi Archive source ledger - June 1–September 12, 1897

This ledger governs the widened text window. It extends the accepted August 23–September 12 window rather than duplicating it.

## Chronology guard

- **June 16, 1897:** an annexation treaty was signed in Washington by representatives of the United States and the Republic of Hawaiʻi.
- **June 17, 1897:** Liliʻuokalani delivered a formal protest against ratification.
- **1897 is an annexation crisis, not completed U.S. annexation.** Formal U.S. annexation followed the Newlands Resolution in 1898, with sovereignty transferred August 12, 1898.
- Feed wording must therefore say treaty, proposed annexation, annexation crisis, resistance, ratification, or similar evidence-bounded language. Do not describe Hawaiʻi as having become U.S. territory in June 1897.

## Accepted pre-treaty information-world record

### June 7 - Ka Ahalono o Hawaii begins

- *Ka Wai Ola*, February 2009, **Kela Mea Kela Mea**, Ronald Williams Jr.:
  https://kawaiola.news/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/KA_WAI_OLA_200902.pdf

Williams's archival column says on June 11, 1897 that "this past Monday" William White had started a new newspaper, *Ka Ahalono o Hawaii*. June 11, 1897 was a Friday, so the reported Monday is June 7.

This is **supported secondary archival reconstruction**, not a recovered first-issue transcription. The feed therefore preserves only the Hawaiian newspaper title in the source-facing field and summarizes the launch event in modern wording. It does not invent an article, editorial voice, or first-person post from William White.

This record is especially useful for the product because it shows the information system itself changing before the treaty: another Hawaiian-language publication enters the public attention network.

## Accepted June treaty/protest tranche

### Liliʻuokalani protest and treaty context

Authority lanes:

- UH Mānoa Library, **Liliʻuokalani's Protest** - June 17, 1897 archival protest and related Hawaiian-language newspaper evidence:
  https://libweb.hawaii.edu/digicoll/annexation/protest/liliu5.php
- *Ke Aloha Aina*, July 10, 1897, p. 6, Hawaiian transcription of the protest as surfaced by Nūpepa Hawaiʻi:
  https://nupepa-hawaii.com/2014/01/28/the-queens-protest-to-treaty-1897/
- U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, for treaty / formal-annexation chronology:
  https://history.state.gov/milestones/1866-1898/hawaii

Accepted atomic signals in `1897-06-01.json`:

1. June 16 treaty context, surfaced through Liliʻuokalani's documented reaction.
2. June 17 formal protest of ratification.
3. Treaty characterized as a wrong against Native Hawaiian and part-Hawaiian people.
4. Lack of consultation / consent as a central protest argument.
5. Direct request to the U.S. president to withdraw the treaty from further consideration.
6. Separate direct request to the U.S. Senate to refuse approval / ratification.

The two closing institutional requests are deliberately separate records. The source addresses two different decision points and two different U.S. institutions; collapsing them obscures the action structure of the protest.

Information-time rule:

The protest was made June 17 in Washington. *Ke Aloha Aina* printed the Hawaiian protest on July 10. Preserve that 23-day event-to-publication gap rather than making the information network behave like modern social media.

## Accepted July 1 micro-feed

### The Independent, July 1, 1897, p. 3

Primary authority:

- Library of Congress, Chronicling America, *The Independent*, July 1, 1897, p. 3, text/image record:
  https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn85047097/1897-07-01/ed-1/?sp=3&st=text
- Library of Congress page PDF:
  https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/ndnp/hihouml/batch_hihouml_eel_ver01/data/sn85047097/00211100370/1897070101/0012.pdf

One historical newspaper page supports three distinct atomic feed items without manufacturing topical balance:

1. **`SAVED.` editorial status** - the paper says Hawaiʻi still remains unannexed and independent, while explicitly warning that the fight is not yet actually won.
2. **Mariposa harbor notice** - the steamer arrives from San Francisco in 6½ days, takes on 200 tons of coal, and is expected to depart for the Colonies that afternoon.
3. **Local fire report** - a pre-dawn fire on Maunakea Street is brought under control, while Assistant Chief John Clark is seriously injured after falling from the burning building.

This cluster is a model for the project: the same issue can carry politics, shipping, and local emergency attention at once. The archive itself supplies the mix, so no modern topic quota is needed.

The source is English-language. `hawaiian` fields therefore preserve only Hawaiian source-facing proper names such as `Hawaii`, `Honolulu`, and `Maunakea`; no Hawaiian article text is invented.

## Accepted July cultural-political response

### July 3 - He Pule Ola Hawaii / Kumulipo

- Noenoe K. Silva, *Aloha Betrayed: Native Hawaiian Resistance to American Colonialism*, quoting *Ke Aloha Aina* in the anti-annexation struggle:
  https://dokumen.pub/aloha-betrayed-native-hawaiian-resistance-to-american-colonialism-9780822386223.html
- Hawaiʻi State Archives, **Mea Liliʻuokalani Exhibition**, identifying Liliʻuokalani's published translation of the Kumulipo as an 1897 work:
  https://ags.hawaii.gov/archives/online-exhibitions/mea-lili%CA%BBuokalani-exhibition/
- Public-domain digital reproduction of Liliʻuokalani's 1897 translation:
  https://sacred-texts.com/pac/lku/index.htm

Silva quotes *Ke Aloha Aina* reproducing the opening lines of the Kumulipo and appending:

`Po-wale-ho-i-e. Hanau ka po ia Hawaii He Aupuni Moi.`

The newspaper called this **He Pule Ola Hawaii** and explained the Kumulipo's beginning as appropriate to a moment when the people lacked stable government. The July 3, 1897 issue date is supported by Silva's citations to *Ke Aloha Aina* in this passage and by later historical treatments of the same prayer.

The feed keeps this as a publication-level editorial/cultural act. There is no named `voice_actor`. The post's Go deeper links lead to Liliʻuokalani's own 1897 published Kumulipo translation so the reader can move from a short feed card into the historical text behind the reference.
