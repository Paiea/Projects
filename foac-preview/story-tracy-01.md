# Tracy

Cliff was not listening to me.

I knew this because he was standing at the supply counter with his back half-turned, uncapping a blue marker with his teeth while trying to tape a torn label back onto the block bin.

Nobody listening to another adult should be doing three things at once.

Especially not Cliff.

Cliff doing three things at once usually meant one of them was about to become four things and at least one object would disappear forever.

“So after snack,” I said, “I was thinking we could leave the story table open instead of breaking it down.”

“Mm-hm,” Cliff said around the marker.

That was not encouraging.

I set a stack of construction paper on the counter harder than necessary.

“The kids could draw one piece from the story in the morning,” I continued. “Then after lunch we put the drawings out with the puppets. Let them retell it however they want. We photograph whatever they build and use that for the family board.”

Cliff pressed the label flat with the side of his fist.

“Mm,” he said.

I looked at the back of his head.

There were times when working with Cliff was very much like working beside an enthusiastic golden retriever who had somehow passed a background check.

“Never mind,” I said.

He turned immediately.

“What?” Cliff asked.

“Nothing.”

“No. You said never mind.”

“You’re busy.”

“I’m labeling.”

“You’re chewing a marker.”

He took it out of his mouth and inspected it as though the accusation required forensic review.

“I was not chewing it,” he said.

“There are teeth marks.”

“Circumstantial.”

I laughed despite myself and reached for the paper again.

“It’s fine,” I said. “I can figure it out.”

Cliff watched me for a second. Then he pointed the blue marker toward the story table.

“No, I like it,” he said.

I looked up.

“The drawings become the props, right?” Cliff asked. “So we’re not making some whole new teacher-created setup they’re supposed to care about.”

My hand stopped on the paper.

“And if we take pictures while they’re retelling,” he continued, “then the family board isn’t us pretending we know what the important part was. It’s literally whatever they chose.”

I stared at him.

Cliff frowned. “What?”

“You were listening.”

He blinked. “Yeah.”

“You were fixing the bin.”

“I can do two things.”

“You were doing at least four.”

He looked down at the tape in one hand and the marker in the other.

“Okay,” he said. “That’s fair.”

Then he nodded toward the table.

“Do it. I think it’s good.”

That should have been the end of it.

A coworker listened while I talked. Congratulations to him. Put it on a fucking certificate.

I picked up the construction paper and squared the edges against the counter.

Except something in me had already prepared for the other version.

The version where I finished talking, realized I had been talking mostly to myself, and quietly took care of it because that was easier than asking anybody to come back and hear me properly.

Instead Cliff had been listening closely enough to catch the part I had not even been sure was the point.

He reached past me for the tape.

I noticed his hand.

There was blue marker across the side of his thumb. Not much. Just a crooked smear where he had apparently missed the label and colored himself instead.

For some stupid reason, that was what stayed.

Not what he said.

Not even the surprise of being heard.

Blue marker.

His thumb.

Ridiculous.

“Tracy?” Cliff said.

I looked up. “What?”

“You just went somewhere.”

“I’m standing right here.”

“Physically.”

I shoved the tape toward him. “Go finish destroying that marker.”

He grinned. “Yes, ma’am.”
